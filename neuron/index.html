Neuron basics

How a neuron fires

Every brain in this lab, from the worm to the fly, is built from one kind of part: the neuron. Here you can poke a single simulated neuron and watch its voltage. Push it a little and nothing happens. Push it past a tipping point and it fires an action potential, the signal neurons use to talk.

2. What you just saw

Four ideas explain almost everything a single neuron does. Each card loads an experiment into the bench above.

Resting potential

A neuron is a tiny battery

At rest, the inside sits about 65 millivolts more negative than the outside. Pumps and leaky potassium channels keep it there. With no stimulus, the line stays flat.

Threshold

All or nothing

A weak push makes a small bump that fades. Past a tipping point, sodium channels fly open, sodium rushes in, and the voltage shoots up to about +40 mV. A stronger push does not make a bigger spike, just the same spike.

Repolarisation

Up, down, and a dip

Sodium channels shut themselves, potassium channels open and potassium flows out. The voltage falls, overshoots below rest (the dip), then settles. Tick "Show the ion channels" to watch both.

Refractory period

A moment to recover

Right after a spike, the neuron cannot fire again, even if pushed: its sodium channels are still locked shut. A second pulse only works once they recover.

Coding strength

Stronger means faster, not bigger

Because every spike is the same size, a neuron signals "stronger" by firing more often. Hold a steady current on and turn it up.

3. Neurons add up their inputs

Neurons get their pushes from other neurons, through synapses. An excitatory synapse nudges the voltage up; an inhibitory one pulls it down. The receiving neuron adds them all up, and only fires if the total crosses threshold.

Two kinds of adding up. Spatial summation: two inputs arriving at the same moment from different neurons. Temporal summation: inputs arriving close together in time, before the first bump has faded. Try a time between them of 0, 3 and 20 ms.

5. From one neuron to a whole brain

The fly brain in this lab is 139,255 neurons, each following the simplified rule you can switch on above: add up the inputs, fire when the voltage crosses a threshold, reset, rest for a moment. Each synapse adds a fixed push (0.275 mV) for every synaptic contact, and inhibitory neurons push down instead of up. That is enough to make the simulated fly put out its tongue for sugar and hold it in for bitter.

Why use the simplified neuron? The real one needs four equations and tiny time steps. Doing that for 139,255 neurons would be far slower. The simplified neuron keeps what matters for passing signals along (threshold, firing, timing) and drops the shape of the spike.

What this simulation doesn't tell you. The "real neuron" is the Hodgkin–Huxley model of a squid's giant axon at 6.3 °C. Human and fly neurons have more kinds of channels, branches and timing, so the numbers differ. The synapses here are simple and illustrative. The simplified neuron's input scale was chosen to make the comparison easy to see; it isn't a measured value. This is a model to build intuition, not a recording from a real cell.

Real neuron: Hodgkin & Huxley 1952, J Physiol 117:500, in the standard modern form (rest −65 mV; sodium 120, potassium 36, leak 0.3 mS/cm²; reversal potentials +50, −77, −54.4 mV). Simplified neuron: the leaky integrate-and-fire model used for the fly brain (Shiu et al. 2024, Nature 634:210): rest and reset −52 mV, threshold −45 mV, 20 ms membrane time constant, 2.2 ms refractory period. Strength is injected current in µA/cm². Everything on this page is computed live in your browser (neuron.js).
